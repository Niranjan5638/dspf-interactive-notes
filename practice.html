Practice and Examination Questions

Graded questions with full worked solutions, from recall to exam-hard.

Questions are grouped by chapter and graded. Every one has a full worked solution behind the disclosure triangle — open it only after attempting the question on paper. The recall questions test whether you know the definitions; applied questions test whether you can execute; exam-hard questions test whether you can reason.

Chapter 1 — Complexity

recall State the formal definition of \(f(n) = O(g(n))\) and explain what \(n_0\) is for.

\(f(n) = O(g(n))\) if there exist positive constants \(c\) and \(n_0\) such that \(f(n) \le c\,g(n)\) for all \(n \ge n_0\).

\(n_0\) is the threshold beyond which the bound holds. Asymptotic notation describes behaviour eventually, so a function may exceed the bound for small inputs and still be \(O(g)\). Without \(n_0\) the definition would fail for almost every real algorithm, because lower-order terms dominate at small \(n\).

applied Give the tight bound for: for (i = n; i > 0; i /= 3) for (j = 0; j < n; j++) work();

The outer loop divides by 3 each time, so it runs \(\lceil \log_3 n \rceil\) times. The inner loop runs \(n\) times, independently. Total \(n \log_3 n\), which is \(\Theta(n \log n)\) since the base of the logarithm is a constant factor.

Common wrong answer: \(O(n^2)\), from assuming the outer loop is \(O(n)\).

exam-hard Prove that \(n^2\) is not \(O(n)\).

Suppose it were. Then there exist \(c > 0\) and \(n_0\) with \(n^2 \le c\,n\) for all \(n \ge n_0\). Divide both sides by \(n\) (positive for \(n \ge n_0 \ge 1\)): \(n \le c\) for all \(n \ge n_0\).

But \(n\) grows without bound, so choose any \(n > \max(c, n_0)\) and the inequality fails. Contradiction, so no such \(c\) exists. ∎

Chapter 2 — Stacks, queues and linked lists

applied A circular queue of capacity 6 has front = 4, rear = 1, count = 3. Which indices hold the three elements?

The elements occupy indices front, front+1, front+2 modulo 6: 4, 5, 0. So indices 4, 5 and 0 hold the queue in FIFO order — index 4 is the oldest (front) and index 0 the newest.

Check: after three dequeues from index 4, front becomes 1, which equals rear. With count = 0 the queue is empty; without the counter you could not distinguish that from full.

exam-hard Explain why an array-based stack doubles rather than growing by a constant, using an amortised argument.

Doubling: copies occur at sizes 1, 2, 4, …, n. Total copy work is \(\sum 2^k < 2n\). Over \(n\) pushes the total is at most \(3n\), so amortised \(O(1)\) per push.

Constant growth by k: a copy of the current size happens every \(k\) pushes. Total work is roughly \(\sum_{i=1}^{n/k} i k \approx n^2/2k\), so amortised \(O(n)\) per push.

The exponential policy makes the copy cost grow geometrically while the number of copies shrinks geometrically, and the two cancel. Constant growth makes the copy cost grow linearly while the frequency stays fixed, and they do not cancel.

Chapter 3 — Trees

applied A binary tree has preorder 4 2 1 3 6 5 7 and inorder 1 2 3 4 5 6 7. Reconstruct the tree.

The first element of preorder is the root: 4. In the inorder sequence, 4 splits the rest into left 1 2 3 and right 5 6 7.

In preorder the next three elements 2 1 3 belong to the left subtree; their first is 2, which splits 1 2 3 into 1 and 3. So 2 has children 1 and 3.

Similarly 6 5 7 gives root 6 with children 5 and 7. The tree is: root 4, left 2 (1, 3), right 6 (5, 7).

exam-hard Prove that a full binary tree with L leaves has exactly 2L − 1 nodes.

Let \(n\) be the total nodes, \(i\) the internal nodes and \(L\) the leaves, so \(n = i + L\).

Count edges from below: every node except the root has exactly one parent, so there are \(n - 1\) edges. Count from above: every internal node has exactly 2 children in a full binary tree, so there are \(2i\) edges. Therefore \(n - 1 = 2i\).

Substituting \(i = n - L\): \(n - 1 = 2(n - L)\), so \(n - 1 = 2n - 2L\), giving \(n = 2L - 1\). ∎

Chapter 4 — Binary search trees

applied Insert 50, 30, 70, 20, 40, 60, 80, then delete 50. Show the tree after each step of the deletion.

Before deletion the tree is balanced: 50 at the root, 30 and 70 as children, 20 and 40 under 30, 60 and 80 under 70.

50 has two children, so this is case 3. Its inorder successor is the minimum of the right subtree: 60. Copy 60 into the root, then delete 60 from the right subtree — where it is a leaf, so case 1.

Result: root 60, left 30 (children 20, 40), right 70 (no left child, right child 80). Inorder still reads 20 30 40 60 70 80.

exam-hard What is the average-case height of a BST built from a random permutation of n distinct keys, and why is the worst case different?

Average case: \(\Theta(\log n)\). With random insertion order the tree behaves like a random binary search tree, whose expected height is about \(4.311 \ln n\) — logarithmic.

Worst case: \(n - 1\), achieved by inserting in sorted order, which produces a chain. Both cases have the same set of keys; only the insertion order differs, so the structure's guarantee is a statement about the input distribution, not about the algorithm.

This is exactly why AVL trees add an invariant: they convert the average-case guarantee into a worst-case one.

Chapter 5 — AVL trees

applied Insert 3, 2, 1, 4, 5, 6, 7 into an empty AVL tree, showing every rotation.

Insert 3, then 2 (left), then 1 (left of 2) → node 3 has bf +2, LL case → right rotation → root 2 with children 1 and 3.

Insert 4 → right child of 3, balanced. Insert 5 → right of 4; node 3 now has bf −2, RR case → left rotation about 3 → subtree becomes 4 with children 3 and 5.

Insert 6 → right of 5; node 4 has bf −2 → left rotation about 4 → 5 becomes subtree root.

Insert 7 → node 5 unbalanced, RR → left rotation → root 4, right 6 with children 5 and 7. Final height 2 for 7 nodes.

exam-hard Prove that an AVL tree of height h has at least n(h) = n(h−1) + n(h−2) + 1 internal nodes, and hence height O(log n).

Take a minimal AVL tree of height \(h\). Its root has two subtrees, one of height \(h-1\) (else the tree would be shorter) and one of height at least \(h-2\) (else the balance factor would exceed 1). Each is itself a minimal AVL tree of its height, so

\(n(h) = n(h-1) + n(h-2) + 1\), with \(n(1) = 1\) and \(n(2) = 2\).

This is the Fibonacci recurrence shifted: \(n(h) = F_{h+2} - 1\). Fibonacci numbers grow as \(\phi^h/\sqrt5\) with \(\phi \approx 1.618\), so \(n(h) = \Omega(\phi^h)\). Inverting, \(h = O(\log_\phi n) = O(\log n)\). ∎

Chapter 6 — Heaps

applied Is [20, 15, 18, 8, 12, 10, 5] a max-heap? If not, fix it with the minimum number of swaps.

Check each parent: 20 ≥ 15, 18 ✓; 15 ≥ 8, 12 ✓; 18 ≥ 10, 5 ✓. Every parent dominates its children, so it is a valid max-heap, and no swaps are needed.

Note that the array is not sorted — 15 and 18 are out of order as siblings. A heap only orders each parent against its own children.

exam-hard Show that heapsort is O(n log n) and uses O(1) extra space, and explain why it is not stable.

Time. build-heap is \(O(n)\). Then \(n-1\) iterations, each a swap plus a sift-down of cost at most \(O(\log n)\). Total \(O(n) + O(n \log n) = O(n \log n)\).

Space. Everything happens inside the input array; only a few index variables are needed, so auxiliary space is \(O(1)\).

Instability. The swap of the root with the last element moves an element from the far end of the array to the front, past any number of equal keys, with no way to preserve their relative order. Stability requires that equal keys never cross, which a long-distance swap violates.

Chapter 7 — Graphs

applied A graph has 7 vertices and 8 edges and is connected. Is it a tree? How many edges must you remove to make it one?

A tree on 7 vertices has exactly 6 edges. With 8 edges the graph is connected and has \(8 - 7 + 1 = 2\) independent cycles, so it is not a tree.

You must remove 2 edges, one from each cycle, to break both without disconnecting the graph.

exam-hard Prove that a connected graph with V vertices and V − 1 edges is acyclic.

Let the graph be connected. Any connected graph on \(V\) vertices has at least \(V-1\) edges — take a spanning tree, which has exactly \(V-1\).

Suppose the graph had a cycle. Removing one edge of that cycle keeps it connected, giving a connected graph with \(V-2\) edges, contradicting the minimum of \(V-1\). Hence no cycle exists. ∎

Chapter 8 — BFS

applied Run BFS from vertex 1 on edges 1–2, 1–3, 2–4, 2–5, 3–6, 3–7, 5–8. Give distances and the BFS tree.

Distances: \(d(1)=0\); \(d(2)=d(3)=1\); \(d(4)=d(5)=d(6)=d(7)=2\); \(d(8)=3\).

BFS tree edges: (1,2), (1,3), (2,4), (2,5), (3,6), (3,7), (5,8).

Visit order: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8. Note that 8 is reached at distance 3 through 5, not through any shorter route.

exam-hard A student claims BFS finds shortest paths in any weighted graph. Refute it.

Counterexample: vertices A, B, C with edges A–B of weight 10, A–C of weight 1, C–B of weight 1.

BFS from A visits C and B at distance 1 edge each, and reports the path A→B at cost 10 as a shortest path because it has the fewest edges. The genuinely cheapest path is A→C→B at cost 2.

BFS minimises the number of edges. To minimise total weight you need a priority queue ordered by accumulated cost — that is Dijkstra's algorithm.

Chapter 9 — DFS

applied Give a topological order for the DAG with edges 1→2, 1→3, 2→4, 3→4. Is it unique?

In-degrees: 1:0, 2:1, 3:1, 4:2. Start with 1 (the only source). Removing it leaves 2 and 3 both at in-degree 0, so either can come next.

Both 1, 2, 3, 4 and 1, 3, 2, 4 are valid. The order is not unique in general — it is unique only when the DAG has a Hamiltonian path.

exam-hard Why does DFS detect cycles in a directed graph but BFS does not, given that both traverse the same edges?

DFS maintains the recursion stack: the set of vertices on the current path from the root. A back edge — an edge to a vertex that is currently grey, meaning still on that path — closes a cycle directly.

BFS processes vertices level by level and has no notion of a current path; when it meets an already-visited vertex it cannot tell whether that vertex is an ancestor or a distant cousin. Detecting a directed cycle with BFS requires extra machinery, which is why DFS is the standard tool.

Chapter 10 — Minimum spanning trees

applied Run Kruskal on edges A–B 4, A–C 2, B–C 1, B–D 5, C–D 8, C–E 10, D–E 2, D–F 6, E–F 3. Give the MST and its weight.

Sorted: BC 1, AC 2, DE 2, EF 3, AB 4, BD 5, DF 6, CD 8, CE 10.

Accept BC, AC, DE, EF. Reject AB (cycle A-B-C-A). Accept BD, which joins the two components.

Five edges for six vertices: {BC, AC, DE, EF, BD}, total \(1+2+2+3+5 = 13\).

exam-hard Prove the cut property: the lightest edge crossing any cut belongs to some MST.

Let \(e\) be the lightest edge crossing cut \((S, V-S)\), and let \(T\) be an MST not containing \(e\). Adding \(e\) to \(T\) creates a unique cycle. That cycle crosses the cut an even number of times, so it contains at least one other crossing edge \(f \ne e\).

By the choice of \(e\), \(w(e) \le w(f)\). Then \(T' = T - f + e\) is a spanning tree — removing a cycle edge preserves connectivity and acyclicity — and \(w(T') = w(T) - w(f) + w(e) \le w(T)\).

Since \(T\) was minimum, \(w(T') = w(T)\), so \(T'\) is also an MST and it contains \(e\). ∎
