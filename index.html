Start here

The chapters follow the lecture order, because each one depends on the last. Complexity gives you the language for comparing everything that follows. Stacks and queues are the simplest containers. Trees generalise a linked list. A binary search tree imposes order on a tree so search becomes fast. AVL trees fix the case where that order collapses. Heaps give up full ordering to keep only the maximum at the root. Graphs generalise trees by allowing cycles, and the last three chapters are about traversing them.

If you have twenty minutes

Read chapter 1 and chapter 6. Complexity is the vocabulary; heaps are the cleanest example of a structure designed around one operation. Between them they teach you how to think about the rest.

What this covers, and what it does not

The course is defined by ten lecture sessions: 01 complexity, 03 stacks and queues, 05 trees, 06 binary search trees, 08 heaps, 10 search trees and AVL, 13 graphs, 14 BFS, 15 DFS and 16 minimum spanning trees. Lectures 02, 04, 07, 09, 11 and 12 do not exist for this course.

Two consequences follow, and they matter for revision. There is no general sorting chapter — the only sorting taught is heapsort, inside chapter 6. And there is no hashing chapter; hash tables appear once, as a passing mention in the BST material. Material outside this scope is marked supplementary wherever it appears.
